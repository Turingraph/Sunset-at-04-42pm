Yes — **especially if your goal is to begin with comprehensible input rather than literacy**.

 Spanish has a major advantage: **you can essentially start listening immediately without first learning a new writing system**. The Latin alphabet, punctuation, and spelling conventions are already familiar enough that you can postpone serious literacy study.

 For comparison:

 - **Japanese** → kana + kanji are a substantial additional learning burden.
- **Chinese** → you can learn to listen without characters, but literacy is a completely separate major undertaking.
- **Arabic** → you have to deal with a different script, plus dialect vs. Modern Standard Arabic issues.
- **Persian** → new script, although the language itself is Indo-European.
- **Russian** → Cyrillic is relatively easy, but still another script to acquire.
- **Korean** → Hangul is actually quite systematic and learnable, but still adds an initial task.
- **Myanmar** → completely different script.
- **Vietnamese** → Latin alphabet, so this advantage is shared with Spanish, but Vietnamese pronunciation/tones are much more demanding for many learners.
- **Khmer** → a new and relatively complex writing system.
- **Thai** → new script plus a tonal system and other pronunciation challenges.

 So Spanish has a particularly nice combination:

 > **familiar alphabet + no tones + relatively transparent spelling + huge amount of comprehensible-input material + lots of global exposure.**

 And there's an interesting consequence for your **Dreaming Spanish** plan.

 You don't actually need to make “learning to read Spanish” a prerequisite at all. You could do something like:

 **Phase 1:**\
 🎧 Mostly listening → Dreaming Spanish

 **Phase 2:**\
 🎧 Listening + gradually learn to read Spanish

 **Phase 3:**\
 🎧 Listening + 📖 reading \+ eventually speaking/writing

 That is very different from languages where the beginner naturally feels:

 > “Before I can even interact with written material, I need to learn this entirely new script.”

 With Spanish, you can immediately see something like **“Hola, me llamo Juan”** and already recognize the basic alphabet. Your cognitive load can therefore be concentrated much more heavily on **the language itself**.

 One caveat, though: **not needing to learn a new script doesn't mean Spanish is dramatically easier in every dimension.** Once you get beyond the beginner stage, Spanish has its own substantial difficulties — verb conjugations, gender, ser/estar, object pronouns, subjunctive, clitic placement, regional variation, and especially the enormous amount of listening practice needed to understand naturally spoken Spanish.

 But for **getting started**, yes. Spanish is unusually friendly.

 In fact, your proposed approach is almost tailor-made for exploiting that advantage: **learn just enough pronunciation and basic language structure to make the first input comprehensible, then let the input do the heavy lifting.**
